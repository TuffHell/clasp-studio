What this is

CLASP is a robot that climbs the frame it is building. It grips a stick, pulls itself up, picks up the next one, and holds it in place while it is fastened — so nobody has to stand on a scaffold. This page simulates it.

The simulation is allowed to fail

Most project demos are animations: they show the thing working because that is what was drawn. This one runs the structural mathematics first and animates the answer, whatever it is. If you draw a frame that cannot stand, it will not stand — it folds, snaps, and lands on the ground.

Two separate engines are doing that work:

1. The analysis — direct stiffness method

Every stick is a bar element with a real cross-section, a real Young's modulus, and real self-weight. The solver assembles a global stiffness matrix, applies the supports, and solves for the movement of every joint. From that it recovers the axial force in every stick, then checks each one three ways: crushing, Euler buckling, and whether the joint itself will pull apart.

If the frame is not braced enough, that matrix becomes singular — mathematically, the structure has a way to move without stretching anything. The solver detects it and names the joint that can swing free. That is not a bug being caught. That is the definition of a mechanism.

2. The collapse — progressive failure plus position-based dynamics

Press Test to failure and the load ramps up. At each level the analysis re-runs, finds the most overloaded stick, breaks it, and then solves the structure again without it. Removing one member pushes its load onto its neighbours, which is often enough to overload the next one. That cascade is the failure sequence, and it is computed, not choreographed.

The falling is done by a position-based dynamics solver: joints become particles, sticks become distance constraints, and gravity does the rest. Broken sticks detach and tumble as loose debris. Nothing is keyframed — if it lands in a heap, that is where the mathematics put it.

How the robot actually moves

The machine in this simulation is the real CAD. It is two chassis halves clamped to the same beam, joined by a four-bar linkage, each half driven along the timber by powered rollers.

Rollers alone cannot get past a crossing member — the beam is blocked. So the halves take turns stepping over it:

StepWhat happens
RollBoth halves grip and the rollers drive the whole robot along the beam.
LiftThe leading half reaches the crossing. The four-bar swings, lifting it clear of the timber.
AdvanceThe trailing half — still clamped — hauls the lifted half forward, past the obstruction.
Set downThe four-bar lowers the leading half onto the beam on the far side of the cross, and it clamps.
SwapNow anchored ahead, that half lifts the trailing one and pulls it over in turn.

One half is always clamped. That is the safety property the whole gait is built around, and it is enforced in the simulation: across four thousand simulated frames, the two halves are never lifted at the same time. A power cut at any instant leaves the robot hanging on a clamp rather than falling.

The arm and claw that place new sticks are the simulator's own placeholder, drawn riding on top of the real chassis. The four-bar swing is animated from pivots measured where the parts touch in the assembly — the servo pivot is a zero-gap contact, so that one is certain; the swing axis is inferred rather than read from the CAD mates, so treat the exact linkage geometry as indicative.

Why the joints are the weak point

Switch between pine and bamboo with a load applied and watch the governing failure mode. Bamboo is the stiffer material, but a wire twist-tie lashing holds roughly half what a screwed joint does — so the bamboo frame fails first, at the tie, not in the wood. That result is the argument for testing tie strength on a bench, and it falls straight out of the model.

What the model does not know

Every joint here is a frictionless pin. Real screwed and lashed joints carry bending moment, so a real half-built frame is stiffer than this simulation says. That is why a part-built frame often shows as "not yet self-stable" — under pin assumptions it genuinely is not, and something has to brace it until the triangle closes. On the real machine, that something is the robot's own clamp.
  • Material and joint strengths are literature values for model-scale stock, not measured ones.
  • Sticks are straight and identical. Real timber is bowed and real bamboo is tapered — handling that variation is the whole point of the vision system, and it is not modelled here.
  • The collapse animation is a computed failure sequence played back, not a moment-by-moment dynamic buckling analysis.
  • Wind, temperature, moisture, and fatigue are all absent.

Project CLASP

A climbing assembly robot for timber and bamboo structures, built for Regeneron ISEF 2027. Falls are the leading cause of construction deaths, and the most exposed task on any frame is holding a member steady at height while somebody fastens it. This machine goes after that one task.
